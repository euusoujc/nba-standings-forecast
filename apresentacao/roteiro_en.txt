>> Slide 1 — Title (10 s) · 0:10
"Good afternoon. Our project is called fifteen games are not a season: how to forecast the final NBA standings when the season has barely started."

>> Slide 2 — The question (15 s) · 0:25
"Right now Philadelphia is 14 and 3. Is that a contender, or just a hot streak? Our question: with about 15 games played, what will each team's final win percentage be, and what are its odds of finishing top 6, in the play-in, or out?"

>> Slide 3 — Data (20 s) · 0:45
"We used nba_api to download 16 seasons, 480 team-seasons. For each one we computed six features using only the first 15 games: win percentage, point differential, net rating, Pythagorean expectation, strength of schedule and last season's record. The target is final win percentage, not wins, because some seasons had fewer than 82 games."

>> Slide 4 — Regression to the mean (30 s) · 1:15
"This plot is the core idea. On the x-axis, win percentage after 15 games; on the y-axis, the final one. If early records were reliable, the points would sit on the diagonal. But the red line has a slope of 0.63: every point above .500 early on is worth only 63% at the end. Early records regress to the mean, so a good model has to shrink extreme starts."

>> Slide 5 — What we tried (20 s) · 1:35
"We compared two baselines, keeping the current win percentage and the Pythagorean formula, against four models: linear regression, Ridge, Lasso and random forest, all with standardization inside the pipeline."

>> Slide 6 — Validation (20 s) · 1:55
"Our unit of validation is the season: we hold out one full season at a time, and we also test on the three most recent ones. Ridge's alpha is chosen by nested cross-validation, also grouped by season."

>> Slide 7 — Results (25 s) · 2:20
"The result: keeping the current win percentage misses by 7.6 wins on average. Ridge misses by 5.6. That's two wins of error gone, and the ranking improves too. To be honest, all four models tied within one standard error. What matters is shrinking the early record, not the algorithm."

>> Slide 8 — Why Ridge (25 s) · 2:45
"So why Ridge? Point differential and net rating correlate at 0.999. Linear regression gives one +0.16 and the other −0.18, which makes no sense. Ridge splits the weight evenly. Same error, coefficients we can interpret: that's the multicollinearity problem from class."

>> Slide 9 — Simulation (25 s) · 3:10
"To get probabilities, we use Ridge's forecast as each team's rating. A logistic regression gives the win probability for every game from the rating gap and home court. Then we simulate the 952 remaining games ten thousand times, with uncertainty in the ratings."

>> Slide 10 — Backtest (20 s) · 3:30
"But a simulation is only worth something if it's calibrated. We replayed the 16 past seasons, out of sample. The 80% interval was right 81% of the time, and the Brier score dropped from 0.66 to 0.42. The probabilities can be trusted."

>> Slide 11 — 2026-27 projection (30 s) · 4:00
"And this is today's projection. Boston has a 97% chance of a top-6 finish, San Antonio 95%. And Philadelphia at 14 and 3? It projects to 52 wins, because its point differential is modest. The model keeps the hype in check."

>> Slide 12 — Lessons (25 s) · 4:25
"What worked: validating by season, with no leakage, and testing the simulation instead of trusting it. The gain is largest very early: after 5 games, the error is cut almost in half. Next time, we'd model injuries and trades, update ratings game by game, and use the official tiebreakers."

>> Slide 13 — Closing (15 s) · 4:40
"To sum up: trust the record, but shrink it first. Regularized regression plus simulation turns 15 noisy games into calibrated odds. The code is in the notebook. Thank you!"

LIKELY QUESTIONS (SHORT ANSWERS)
--------------------------------

  * Why not a neural network or something more complex? With 480 observations and an almost linear relationship, the random forest already gave no gain. More complexity would only add variance.
  * Why Ridge if OLS had a lower MAE? The gap (0.12 wins) is smaller than the between-season standard error (0.25). We picked the most stable model with the one-standard-error rule.
  * What about injuries? They're not in the model; that's the main limitation. Ratings come from results only.
  * Why 15 games? It's the fewest games any team has played today. The sensitivity analysis covers N = 5 to 41.
  * Are the tiebreakers real? No, they're random. Official NBA tiebreakers are future work.
